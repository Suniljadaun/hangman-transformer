# Hangman — a masked-word transformer trained on its own board states

A Hangman agent for the Meltwater *Brand & Buzzword* hackathon. Six wrong guesses
per word, a 250,000-word test set, and a training vocabulary that shares **zero
words** with it — so the agent has to learn English structure, not a word list.

**Public leaderboard: 50.5752 → 70.0334.**

Final submission: `hangman_r3.pt` at `fusion=0.6` — a 10-layer, `d_model=384`
encoder trained from scratch on 9,167,185 aggregated on-policy board states.

---

## The problem, stated correctly

Five facts about this competition determine the entire design. Getting any of
them wrong costs more than any amount of tuning recovers.

**The dictionaries are disjoint.** Verified, not assumed: of the 250,000 test
words, exactly 0 appear among the 225,300 training words. Anything that relies
on recognising the word scores nothing. This is measurable — the pattern-filtered
dictionary policy below barely beats a letter-frequency table, because the word
it is looking for is never in its candidate set.

**Misses carry as much information as hits.** Knowing there is no `e` in a
nine-letter word reshapes the posterior enormously. The network is conditioned
on two separate 26-dimensional vectors — letters tried, and letters tried *and
known absent* — whose difference encodes the hits.

**A repeated letter is a strike.** Any character that fails to reveal something
new costs a life, so guess strings must be strictly de-duplicated. At inference
the logits of already-guessed letters are set to `-inf`; a unit test asserts the
policy can never return one.

**The local number is the leaderboard number.** Guess sequences are generated by
simulating against the public word list, so an honest simulation predicts the
score before submitting. Measured three times:

| local | public | residual |
|---|---|---|
| 50.799 | 50.5752 | −0.22 |
| 67.776 | 67.9306 | +0.15 |
| 70.097 | 70.0334 | −0.06 |

The residuals bracket zero, so the estimate is unbiased and worth about ±0.25.
Every design decision in this repo was judged against the local number; the
leaderboard was only ever used to confirm it.

**Training on states the policy never visits teaches the wrong problem.** This
is the one that matters most, and it is the subject of the next section.

## Results

Every number is a win rate from an honest simulation — the policy sees the
masked board, the guess history and the lives remaining, and nothing else.

| stage | holdout (10k) | test.txt (250k) | public LB |
|---|---|---|---|
| length-conditioned letter frequency | 13.03% | — | — |
| pattern-filtered dictionary | ~17% | — | — |
| character n-gram back-off | 48.50% | 50.799% | 50.5752 |
| transformer, round 0 (heuristic states) | 50.22% | — | — |
| transformer, round 1 (+ on-policy) | 63.32% | — | — |
| transformer, round 2 (+ on-policy) | 65.96% | 67.776% | 67.9306 |
| **transformer, round 3 (wider, 9.2M states)** | **67.28%** | **70.097%** | **70.0334** |

Mean strikes per word fell alongside: 4.567 → 3.627 → 3.433. That matters
because the competition breaks ties on total wrong guesses.

The holdout is 10,000 words held out of `train.txt` and disjoint from every
state used in training. `test.txt` is used only to simulate games — never to
build a vocabulary, a frequency table or an n-gram count.

### What each idea was worth

| step | gain |
|---|---|
| n-gram back-off over letter frequency | **+35.5** |
| transformer on heuristic-generated states, over the n-gram | **+1.7** |
| on-policy state aggregation, rounds 0 → 3 | **+17.1** |

That middle row is the finding worth reading twice. A transformer trained the
obvious way — states produced by someone else's policy — barely beat a
statistical baseline. Almost the entire contribution of the neural approach came
from fixing *what it was trained on*, not from the architecture.

### Where the wins came from

Win rate by word length on `test.txt`:

| length | 4 | 6 | 8 | 9 | 10 | 12 | 15 | 18 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| n-gram | 12.99 | 21.49 | 37.71 | 46.95 | 57.15 | 75.06 | 90.55 | 95.64 |
| round 3 | 15.11 | 34.01 | 62.14 | 74.54 | 83.47 | 93.84 | 98.57 | 99.15 |

An n-gram sees a four-character window. On a fifteen-letter word that window
almost always straddles enough revealed letters to pin down the next one, which
is why long words were already at 90%. On an eight-letter word with two letters
showing it is guessing nearly blind. The transformer reads the whole word and
learns that `_ _ _ _ i n g` and `u n _ _ _ _ _` constrain each other, so the
gains land where the local model was weakest: **+24 points at length 8, +28 at
length 9**, and almost nothing at length 18 where there was nothing left to win.

Losses, as a share of all words still lost:

| band | n-gram | round 3 |
|---|---|---|
| 2–6 letters | 25.9% | **38.1%** |
| 7–9 letters | 48.5% | **48.7%** |
| 10–12 letters | 21.7% | 12.0% |
| 13+ letters | 3.8% | 1.2% |

Words of 13 letters or more are effectively solved. What remains is 87%
concentrated in words of nine letters or fewer, and much of that is at a real
ceiling rather than a tuning failure: `train.txt` holds 2,184 distinct
three-letter words and `test.txt` holds 1,429 *different* ones, so with zero
overlap the test set's short words are essentially what is left over after a
225,000-word dictionary — fragments with little English structure to learn.

## The method

### Architecture

The board is a character sequence with most letters hidden. A pre-LN transformer
encoder reads it, conditioned on the guess history, and two heads answer the same
question differently.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Input** | 29-token vocabulary: `[PAD]`, `[MASK]`, `a–z`, `[OTHER]` for revealed non-letters |
| **Conditioning** | 26-dim *tried* and 26-dim *absent* multi-hot, plus lives remaining and word length, projected to `d_model` and added to every token |
| **Backbone** | 10 layers, `d_model=384`, 8 heads, FFN 1536, dropout 0.1 (rounds 0–2 used 8 layers at 256) |
| **Head A** | per-position softmax over 26 letters, aggregated across hidden positions by noisy-OR: `P(ℓ present) = 1 − Π(1 − pᵢ(ℓ))` |
| **Head B** | pooled bag-of-letters head, 26 logits under BCE |
| **Fusion** | blended in logit space at `0.6`, tuned on the holdout; tried letters masked to `-inf` |
| **Loss** | masked-LM cross entropy on hidden positions + BCE on letters still to find |

Head A is what lets the model exploit morphology — `-ing`, `-tion`, `un-`,
`-ness` are position-dependent facts that a bag-of-letters head cannot represent.
Head B is more robust where A is confidently wrong. Neither dominates: on the
full holdout the fusion sweep peaks at 0.6 on both win rate and strikes, with
0.0 costing 1.0 point and 1.0 costing 0.6.

> A note on tuning honestly: an earlier sweep run on 4,000 holdout words picked
> `fusion=0.3`. At that sample size the standard error is ~0.8 points, so every
> value was statistically indistinguishable and the "best" pick was noise. On the
> full 10,000 the curve has a clean single peak at 0.6.

### Training on the boards the policy actually reaches

Masking letters at random produces boards no real game visits. A real game
reaches states shaped by the policy's own choices: vowels revealed early, common
consonants tried, several specific letters known absent. Training on the wrong
distribution costs about seventeen points here, so states are generated
on-policy and aggregated across rounds — dataset aggregation applied to a word
game.

```
Round 0   play all 215,300 training words with the n-gram heuristic
          → log every (word, guess-mask, lives) state
          → train v0                            2.3M states, holdout 50.22%

Round 1   replay with v0 (ε-mixed for exploration)
          → aggregate, retrain from scratch     4.7M states, holdout 63.32%

Round 2   replay with v1 → aggregate → retrain  7.0M states, holdout 65.96%

Round 3   replay with v2 → aggregate → retrain  9.2M states, holdout 67.28%
          (wider model: 10 layers at d_model=384)
```

Two details matter. Earlier rounds' states are kept rather than replaced, and
each round retrains from scratch rather than fine-tuning, so the new model does
not inherit the previous policy's blind spots.

Exploration is deliberately gentle: `epsilon=0.02, noise=0.05` costs the
generating policy about five points of win rate. Stronger settings were measured
and rejected — `noise=0.15` took the n-gram policy from 54% to 30%, which widens
the state distribution by filling it with boards a competent policy never reaches.

**Where this stops.** The gap between a model's win rate on training words and on
the holdout widened each round: +4.6 after round 1, +6.8 after round 2. That is
the buffer beginning to teach the model about *these specific words* rather than
about English, and it is why round 3 was the last. Round 3's gains were also
concentrated in lengths 7–14 while lengths 4–5 slipped slightly — the two effects
are converging, and a round 4 would likely trade more short-word accuracy than it
gained elsewhere.

### Engineering notes

A board state is fully determined by the word plus the set of letters guessed,
so the buffer stores nine bytes per state — a word index, a 26-bit mask and the
lives remaining — and reconstructs tokens and labels on the fly. Nine million
states cost about eighty megabytes.

Batches are built vectorised and length-bucketed rather than one item at a time.
Word lengths run from 1 to 29, so naive batching would spend most of the
sequence budget on padding, and a per-item loader would starve the GPU.

## Layout

```
src/hangman/
  encoding.py     token vocabulary, packing, board rendering
  data.py         word-list loading, auditing, disjoint holdout split
  simulator.py    batched no-peeking simulator — the secret word lives here and nowhere else
  model.py        HangmanNet: transformer + MLM head + word head
  states.py       on-policy state collection and vectorised batching
  train.py        AdamW + cosine schedule + AMP
  evaluate.py     competition metrics and the per-length breakdown
  submit.py       submission writing and the validator that catches silent point leaks
  policies/
    base.py       Policy interface; guarantees a letter is never repeated
    frequency.py  length-conditioned letter frequency
    ngram.py      character n-gram back-off with noisy-OR aggregation
    dictionary.py pattern-filtered candidate voting, memoised on board state
    neural.py     the learned policy
    ensemble.py   score fusion and the exploration wrapper
notebooks/
  00_baseline_ngram.ipynb   self-contained n-gram baseline, CPU, ~12 min → 50.799%
  01_train.ipynb            Kaggle GPU: rounds 0–2, ~1.5 h on a T4
  02_submit.ipynb           inference only, writes submission.csv
  03_round3.ipynb           round 3 on a wider model, ~2 h → 70.097%
tests/                      8 tests: no peeking, no repeats, valid submission, learning
```

## Running it

```bash
pip install -r requirements.txt
python -m pytest tests -q          # 8 passed
```

Training runs on Kaggle. Everything is reproducible from `train.txt` alone;
`test.txt` is used only to simulate games.

### On the tests

`tests/test_learning.py` asserts that after training on a handful of words the
network picks a letter the word still needs, on those same states, essentially
every time. It deliberately does **not** assert a game win rate on those words:
a model trained on one policy's trajectories and then asked to follow its own
visits states it never saw, and on twelve words there is nothing to generalise
from. That gap is the whole reason the training loop aggregates on-policy states,
and folding it into a unit test would make the test measure the wrong thing.

## Integrity

- The simulator is the only object that holds a secret word. Policies receive an
  `Observation` — masked board, guess history, lives — and there is no code path
  that puts a word into one.
- `test.txt` is never used to fit anything. Training and holdout words come from
  `train.txt` only.
- No per-word special-casing and no cached answers: the policy is a pure function
  of the observation.
- `submit.validate_submission` fails loudly on repeated letters, misaligned
  `word_id`s, and wrong row counts.
